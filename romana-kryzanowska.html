…because Pilates.

Romana Kryzanowska

June 30, 1923 – August 30, 2013

As a mother of four, I recognize Romana in more than the exercises. I recognize her in running a business while your kids’ lives never stop moving either.

The teacher who carried the classical repertoire forward — and the line Therapilatics trained in.

Romana Kryzanowska was sixteen and training at George Balanchine’s School of American Ballet when an ankle injury threatened to end her dancing before it started. Balanchine didn’t send her to a surgeon. He took her by the hand to a small studio on Eighth Avenue and introduced her to a man he called a genius of the body: Joseph Pilates.

Joe told her to take five lessons — three the first week, two the second — and promised her money back if her ankle wasn’t better. By the third lesson, the pain was gone. She went back to ballet within days. She also, without quite meaning to, never really left the studio. She started as a client, became a helper, and within a few years was training alongside Joseph and Clara Pilates as one of their own.

In 1944 she married a Peruvian businessman and moved to Lima, where she raised two children and kept teaching the method out of her home. When her daughter developed a leg condition, Joseph Pilates — thousands of miles away — designed an exercise specifically for her; the Kryzanowska family still called it “the Sari.” Romana made regular trips back to New York to train with “Uncle Joe and Aunt Clara” in person, then returned to the city for good in the late 1950s, working alongside them at the studio and teaching ballet on the side.

Joseph Pilates died in 1967. Clara kept the studio running for a few more years, and in 1970 Romana Kryzanowska took over as its director — becoming the person most responsible for carrying the exact repertoire, order, and teaching style of the original method into the next fifty years. For the rest of her life, she trained the teachers who trained the next generation of teachers, insisting on precision: this exercise, in this order, taught this way, because that’s how Joe and Clara taught it to her.

Romana also left her own quiet mark on the shape of the class itself. Joe’s own approach was famously brisk — he liked to say a session should run to about 45 minutes, “an hour, in the shower,” done and out the door with time to spare. Romana, teaching her own clients for a full hour block, found 45 minutes didn’t fill it. She started running her own sessions closer to 55 minutes instead, and the length stuck — a practical fix born of a full appointment book that became tradition.

She’s also widely credited with two additions to the classical mat repertoire that don’t appear in Joe’s own book, Return to Life Through Contrology: the straight-leg versions of the leg-stretch work, done with the legs extended rather than bent. Alongside Joe’s originals, they became known as the “Series of Five” — five ways of working the same muscles from different angles, still taught that way in classical studios today. Not everyone agrees she’s the one who added them — some of her own longtime students insist she changed almost nothing about the original method — but the credit to her is the version most commonly taught.

Therapilatics’ own training runs through her line — Third Degree, classical, traced back through Real Pilates New York to the studio Romana ran on Eighth Avenue. The reformer sequence you’re doing here carries her fingerprints, and so does the clock: a full session still runs 55 minutes, the way Romana built it.

This shirt exists because someone spent sixty years making sure the original work didn’t get lost — and Therapilatics trained in the line she left behind.

The Mythology

Pilates history runs thick with oral tradition — stories passed teacher to student for sixty years, closer to folklore than footnote. One that’s often told about Romana’s straight-leg additions: that she built them specifically to target the lower-belly “pooch” every mother recognizes, the stubborn softness bent-knee ab work doesn’t reach. It’s a good story, and it’s anatomically plausible — straight-leg raises do recruit the lower abdominals harder than bent-knee ones. But it isn’t something documented in her own words, so take it the way it’s handed down: as legend, not history.
